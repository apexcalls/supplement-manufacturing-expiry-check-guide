How to Check a Supplement's Manufacturing Date Against Its Expiry Date

Most supplement bottles print an expiry date, and some also print a manufacturing date. Read together, the two dates tell you something the expiry date alone cannot: how old the product already is, how long it has been sitting on a shelf or in a warehouse, and whether the shelf life the brand claims is realistic. The gap between the two dates is one of the most useful numbers on the whole bottle, and most buyers never calculate it.

The first thing the gap tells you is the product's age. A bottle manufactured in January 2024 with an expiry date of January 2026 has a two-year shelf life, and if you buy it in December 2025, you are buying a product with one month of its guaranteed life left. The expiry date alone does not tell you that — it only tells you the end of the story, not where you are in it. A bottle with a long gap between manufacture and expiry can still be nearly expired when you buy it, and a bottle with a short gap can be fresh. The manufacturing date is the only way to tell which one you are holding.

The second thing the gap tells you is whether the shelf life is honest. Different ingredients degrade at different speeds: a stable mineral can last for years, while a delicate oil or a live probiotic may lose potency in months. A brand that gives a fragile ingredient a three-year shelf life is either using a stabilisation method it should be able to describe, or it is printing a date its product cannot meet. A brand that gives a stable ingredient a one-year shelf life may simply be cautious, or it may be hiding the fact that the product was manufactured long ago and the short life is all that remains. The Supplement Rating explains how to judge shelf-life claims in its label-checking guide, which covers storage, stability, and what an expiry date actually guarantees.

The third thing the gap reveals is the supply chain. A product manufactured two years ago and sold today has spent two years somewhere — in a warehouse, in a distributor's stock, or on a retailer's shelf — and every month in storage is a month of slow degradation, especially if the storage was warm or humid. A product manufactured last month and sold today has spent almost no time in storage, and its dose is far more likely to match its label. The manufacturing date is, in effect, a receipt for how long the product has been waiting for you.

What should a buyer do? When a bottle prints both dates, subtract one from the other and ask three questions. How old is this bottle right now? Is the shelf life plausible for the ingredients inside? And will I finish this bottle before the expiry date, at the dose I actually take? If the answer to the last question is no, buy a smaller bottle, even if it costs more per capsule — a bottle you finish is worth more than a bottle you throw away half full. A gelatin-based formula, for example, should be judged on its freshness as well as its dose, and a review of a gelatin-based formula shows what to look for when the date and the product disagree.

Not every bottle prints a manufacturing date, and its absence is itself information: a brand that prints both dates is showing you its supply chain, and a brand that prints only the expiry date is showing you the minimum the law requires.
